The water path, top to bottom

1

Manual shutoff

A normal ball valve you can close by hand if anything needs to come apart. Received. Male/male. The flow sensor seals on flat faces, so it joins the shutoff through a G½ flat-face union: swivel nut and flat washer on the sensor side, female thread on the shutoff side.

2

Flow sensor

YF-B1-S, stainless, G½. First after the shutoff, so it stays full of water at mains pressure between fills and reads from the first second of a fill. Downstream of the valve it would drain after every fill and start each one with air in the turbine, under-counting until it flooded. Arrow pointing downstream, axis horizontal, a short straight run before it. No PTFE tape on its flat faces.

3

Adjustable restrictor

Male/female, joined to the sensor's outlet by a second flat-face union. Tuned once by hand to 2 L/min. Must stay downstream of the sensor: pressure collapses past a restrictor, which is the condition the sensor dislikes. Upstream of the valve it limits the flow through the ball valve while it spends 10 to 15 s partly open, which spares the seat. See "Restricting the flow" below.

4

Electric valve

Stainless body with the 8 Nm 24 V actuator, wired to fail closed. 15 s travel on the label, 10 to 11 s on the bench. The body is interchangeable between the two motors on hand, so this closes with a screwdriver rather than a purchase. When closed it holds full mains pressure regardless of the restrictor: a restrictor only drops pressure while water flows. Leave a straight G½ section or a union directly after it for the future solenoid.

4b

Backup solenoid (deferred)

Directly after the main valve. 24 V DC, normally closed, direct acting (0 bar minimum), 8 W or less. Wired in parallel with the valve's open winding at J9 OPEN and COM, through the spare right-wall gland, so it opens and closes with every fill and shuts in under a second. A float trip drops both relays, so it closes without the ESP32. No PCB change. Spec in BOM.md.

5

Inlet through the lid

Through a drilled hole in the lid, ending above the maximum water line. Deliberately not sealed around the pipe: the same hole vents air displaced during a fill. A lid with only a sealed pipe through it burps water back out.

6

Upper chamber

Holds the Black Berkey elements (B1 and B2 fitted, B3 planned), the upper probe and the single float switch. The lower probe's cable also crosses it on its way from the base gland to the lid. Closed by a drilled stainless lid — see "The upper chamber lid" below. Passively drips into the lower chamber the whole time, independent of the fill valve.

7

Lower chamber

Upper chamber's base covers most of its opening, so the lower probe's cable rises through a sealed M16 gland in an 18 mm spare element hole, crosses the upper chamber and leaves through its own hole in the lid. The base hole is below the upper chamber's waterline and must seal properly. Spare holes only exist with 2 or 3 elements installed, not 4.

Why the flow sensor moved to the front. Bench testing showed the sensor needs pressure behind it to read. In a single series line the flow through it is the same on either side of the valve; what differs is what happens between fills. After the valve, the short run drains into the Berkey and the open outlet gives almost no back pressure, so the turbine starts every fill dry. Before the valve, it never empties.

The trade-off, accepted. The sensor now carries mains pressure around the clock, outside the motorised valve's protection. It is built for line pressure; the manual shutoff in front covers maintenance; and it strengthens the case for the deferred floor leak sensor.

A useful side effect. Once the valve has finished closing, any pulses mean the valve is passing, since the sensor is always wet and ready to turn. The firmware's leak check uses exactly that, and skips the valve's closing travel so a normal stop does not trip it.

Mounting the probes

Both probes hang vertically, tip down, with the diaphragm 10 to 15 mm above the chamber floor. Not horizontal, and not resting on the bottom.

A hydrostatic probe measures the pressure of the water column above its diaphragm, so the diaphragm's height is the zero point. Water below it is invisible to the sensor, and a few degrees of tilt shifts that zero by centimetres of reading. Laid sideways the diaphragm also faces into the flow, where air bubbles cling to it. Resting it on the floor is no better: debris collects underneath and the zero moves whenever the probe settles.

The horizontal-mounting rule that exists on this project belongs to the flow sensor, not the probes. Turbine meters need their axis horizontal with a straight run ahead of them or the rotor loads unevenly and undercounts at low flow.

The vent capillary. A thin air tube runs inside the probe's cable jacket, referencing the diaphragm to atmosphere. It must run upward to a dry end. A drooping cable run collects condensation in that tube, which reads as levels drifting downwards over days.

It also sets a limit on how hard any gland can be tightened. Crush the capillary and the probe reads barometric drift instead of depth, or stops responding. Tighten until the cable will not slide by hand, then stop, and confirm the reading still tracks a changing water level afterwards.

Upper chamber — hanging from the lid gland

This reverses an earlier decision. Previous revisions of this document mounted the upper probe on a stainless strip clamped under a filter element's wingnut. It now hangs from its own lead through the lid's M16 gland, the same way the lower probe hangs from the gland in the chamber base.

The objection to lid mounting was that the lid comes off and takes the probe's zero with it. That objection is weaker than it looked: elements last 2 to 10 years, the lid otherwise stays on to keep dust out, and a bracket clamped under a wingnut moves too whenever an element is changed. What the lid route buys is one less fabricated part and one less thing to corrode in the chamber.

Two mitigations are required, not optional.

Mark the cable with a permanent marker where it enters the gland, so one glance says whether it has moved. Leave coiled slack above the lid so it can be lifted and set aside without dragging the probe up with it.

Without both, a lid removal silently invalidates the zero and there is no way to tell afterwards. No software can recalculate the offset on its own — recalibration is two weighed 2 L additions. The one real possibility is cross-checking against the lower probe, which is glanded into the chamber base and genuinely does not move. Worth building once there are real numbers to test it against.

Lower chamber — through the 18 mm hole

Measured: the spare element hole in the upper chamber's base is 18 mm. The probe's sensing head is around 40 mm across, so the body cannot pass through it under any circumstances. The bulkhead-collar option is dead; a cable gland is the only route, and the probe hangs from its own lead.

This port is wet, not dry. An earlier revision of this document said it sealed only against dust and insects. That was wrong. The upper chamber's base is the lower chamber's lid, so this hole sits below the upper chamber's waterline whenever a fill has just run — which is exactly the state the fill logic creates.

Left unsealed it weeps into the lower chamber and bypasses the filter elements entirely, which is worse than a leak. The pressure is trivial, about 0.02 bar at 20 cm of depth, so any IP68 gland handles it — but it has to actually be a seal, with the gland body and its flange O-ring on the upper side and the locknut below. Seal on the pressure side.

The upper chamber lid

The upper chamber is closed by a replacement stainless lid — a 22 cm raised pot cover, drilled with four holes: both probe cables, the water inlet and the float switch. It replaces the original lid rather than modifying it. Drill all four in one setup.

Why lid mounting is fine for the float and costly for the probe. The float has no calibration. It trips in a band centimetres wide, so a few millimetres of lid variation is irrelevant. The probe's whole accuracy is its diaphragm height.

Mount the float below the maximum fill line and above the normal fill target. If it trips during an ordinary fill, the target is too high. Its 350 mm cable needs an extension — solder and heatshrink, joint sited above the lid, never inside the chamber.

Never route the upper probe's cable down through a second hole in the chamber base. That runs the vent capillary downhill into a closed, humid chamber, which is precisely the condensation failure described above. Up and out, always.

Drilling the lid

A pot lid is thin, roughly 0.4 to 0.6 mm. Use a step drill, not a twist bit, which grabs and tears thin stainless. Slow speed, firm pressure, cutting oil, and a scrap of wood backing the sheet. Deburr both sides.

17 mm is the nominal hole for M16; 18 mm also works and makes both probe ports identical, so a spare gland fits either. The gland nut will dimple sheet this thin if tightened against bare metal — the EPDM washers either side spread the load and keep the cable entering straight.

Space the two probe glands at least 40 mm apart, centre to centre, so both nuts and washers fit. Place the inlet hole well away from both probe glands, and set the pipe so its end stays above the maximum water line. An air gap between supply pipe and tank water is worth keeping; a pipe dipping into the chamber is not.

Accepted unknown: the lid's steel grade. Cheap pot lids are often 201 stainless rather than 304. 201 spots and pits in permanently damp service, and the underside of this lid carries condensation all day.

Accepted deliberately rather than chased: anything shedding from it lands in the unfiltered upper chamber and passes through the Black Berkey elements afterwards. The lid is cheap and swappable without touching anything else, so the plan is to fit it and replace it if rust appears. Two were bought — once drilled, it is drilled.

If only 2 elements are fitted there is likely a second spare hole in the chamber base. Confirm its blanking plug is still in — an open hole passes unfiltered water straight through.

Assembly order, and why it matters later

The probe head can never pass through the 18 mm hole, so it lives permanently inside the lower chamber. Build it in this order: probe placed in the lower chamber, lead fed up through the hole from below, gland fitted and tightened from above, upper chamber replaced on top.

That leaves the cable captive in the gland. Every time the upper chamber comes off for cleaning or an element change, either the cable has enough slack to let the upper chamber be lifted clear and set aside — 30 to 40 cm coiled in the lower chamber — or the gland has to be undone, which loses the calibrated zero and means repeating the two weighed additions. Buy the slack.

The lid is now captive too. The lower probe's cable runs through both the base gland and the lid gland, so lifting the lid needs coiled slack above the lid on that cable as well as on the upper probe's. Inside the upper chamber leave it slightly slack between the two glands, never taut: a taut run pulls on the base gland, and that gland sets the lower probe's zero.

The head also makes the probe heavier than a bare-cylinder probe, likely 300 to 500 g. Well within what a gland holds on a 7 mm jacket, but it sharpens the capillary rule: the load cannot be taken by tightening harder. Set the height, snug the gland, and let the coiled slack absorb any tug on the cable. The same now applies to the upper probe, which hangs from the lid gland by its own lead.

Clearance inside the lower chamber has been checked and is not a constraint. The 40 mm dimension is the sensing head at the bottom; the body above it tapers to the cable entry, and the diaphragm sits on the underside of that head — so the 10 to 15 mm clearance is measured from the head's underside, not from anywhere on the body.

Materials

Everything wetted or sitting permanently in a chamber: 304 or 316 stainless, silicone, or EPDM. Not galvanised, not zinc-plated, not aluminium, not brass. Same reasoning already applied to the valve — a gland is cheap enough that there is no reason to compromise. The probe body itself is stainless, so it raises no question of its own.

Calibrate after mounting, not before

Once the probe is finally fixed, add a weighed 2 L, note the reading, then a second weighed 2 L and note it again. Two points give the offset and confirm the 366 mL/cm figure below. Any remount invalidates it — including retightening a gland that lets the probe slip.

Restricting the flow

The valve takes 15 seconds to travel closed by its label, 10 to 11 s on the bench without water pressure. Earlier revisions of this document assumed 5 seconds and every overshoot figure in them is wrong by a factor of three. From the moment the firmware decides to stop, water keeps arriving for the whole of that travel — and since fill volume is bounded by the lower chamber's headroom, that overshoot comes straight out of the safety margin. The table uses the label's 15 s, the worse case. A backup solenoid, if fitted, would cut it to a few millilitres.

The real rate at the lid could be anywhere from 5 to 12 L/min once the shutoff, the restrictor, the valve, the flow sensor and the fittings have each taken their pressure drop. Measure it during the K-factor session and replace both estimates here. Nothing in the design turns on which one is right: at either rate the overshoot is far too large and the restrictor is mandatory.

The part

An adjustable restrictor, male/female, is on order. It sits between the flow sensor and the valve. The flat-face union on the sensor's outlet takes its far end from the restrictor: male if the restrictor's female end is its inlet, female if the male end is. Check the restrictor's flow arrow on the real part before buying that union. Brass is not acceptable here — this sits upstream of drinking water with nothing between it and the filter inlet, exactly the position brass was rejected for on the ball valve.

Take 1/2" to match the DN15 valve and the YF-B1-S's G½, not a smaller body. The needle does the restricting; body size only decides what it threads into, and a 1/4" body means two reducers and two more joints that can weep.

Setting it

Install, open the supply, watch litres per minute in Home Assistant, turn the handle until the rate is right. The flow sensor makes this a two-minute job instead of guesswork, and the same run doubles as the K-factor calibration against a kitchen scale.

Calibrate the K-factor at the rate you will run, not at full bore. The YF-B1-S's ±3 % band runs from 2 to 6 L/min, so 2 L/min sits right on its lower edge, where turbine meters get least linear. If the three calibration runs scatter more than a few percent, set the restrictor to 2.5 L/min instead: inside the band, for about 125 mL more overshoot, still well within the 3.5 cm margin.

Record the handle position and check it occasionally. It is the kind of setting that gets knocked and nobody notices. Do not run a fill unrestricted even once.

Two alternatives considered

Throttling with the manual shutoff instead. Free, and viable as a stopgap. But a ball valve does almost all its flow change in the last 10 to 15 degrees of travel, so the setting is twitchy and easy to knock past; throttling erodes the seat over time, drifting the valve toward not sealing when it matters; and if the shutoff is also the throttle, closing it for maintenance means retuning afterwards.

Partially opening the main valve. Rejected, and it is worth recording why, because it looks attractive. The relay's NC contact feeds the valve's close wire, so de-energising mid-travel does not stop the actuator — it reverses it. Holding a partial position needs a second relay interrupting COM, and with COM open an unpowered system leaves the valve wherever it happened to be, including half open. The actuator has no spring return. That trades the fail-closed property, the single most important decision in the build, for a flow rate a cheap part already provides. Open-loop timing on an actuator with no position feedback, whose speed varies with supply voltage and water torque, would also drift.

Where the electronics sit

Above the tank, on a shelf or bracket, never below it. Cables run down to the valve, flow sensor, float switch and both probes; nothing electrical sits anywhere water could reach it even in a worst-case overflow. A short drip loop in each cable — a low point before it reaches the box — is cheap insurance: any water that tracks down the cable drips off at the low point instead of running into the enclosure.

One board, nothing else in the box (revision G). The carrier board carries the ESP32, the THVD1406 transceiver, the buck module, both relays with their MOSFET drivers, all four flyback diodes and the three PTC fuses. There is no relay module, diode carrier or distribution block any more, and no junction anywhere but on the board. The flyback diodes satisfy the "at the relay's own terminals" rule by sitting next to K1, K2 and J9.
